Chapter-by-Chapter Highlights

Part One — "The Past"
  • Natalie narrates a "Content Day" of filming sourdough tutorials and staged family dinners, all while privately holding her audience, staff, and even her kids in contempt
  • Her daughter Clementine asks what "tradwife" actually means, then later tells Natalie to stop filming me — a first crack in the family's polished image
  • That night, Shannon resigns (Natalie already knows about her affair with Caleb)
  • Caleb reveals his father wants him to run for office — a plan Natalie secretly engineered
Quotes to discuss
"People think they want minimalism […] when in fact a perfectly uncluttered home makes them want to kill themselves. […] most people don't take a moment to really think about anything. Most people are morons."Part One
"I was a shark, and they were five million tiny fish, nipping at the nutrients along my belly."Part One
"Stop filming me."Clementine, Part One
From your highlights
"That's the thing about being a mother and a wife and an influencer, all at the same time: it's basically like breastfeeding three babies simultaneously."Part One, p. 12
"The worst thing you can ever do is let a child know you care."Part One, p. 21

  • Outside, a colder, harder version of Caleb catches her trying to run and slaps her unconscious

Part Three — "The Future"
  • The woman from the Subaru is a grown Clementine, arriving with a production crew to prep Natalie for a televised interview — and the story finally unspools all at once
  • The entire "1855" world was a deliberate hoax that Natalie and Caleb built together, starting after the Shannon assault and lawsuit made the family's legal exposure too dangerous to face; they told a toddler-aged Mary that her older siblings had simply died and "gone to heaven"
  • The two "neighbor" men are revealed to be Natalie's own grown sons, Stetson and Samuel — they've been secretly supplying the ranch with food for years, and it's Stetson who finally calls the police after Natalie stumbles into his cabin mid-escape
  • Clementine reveals Natalie is fifty years old and going through menopause, not pregnant — and that Doug's threat to have her killed in a staged "accident" is what directly pushed Natalie and Caleb into the fantasy in the first place
  • Authorities remove the younger children from the ranch; Natalie and Caleb, alone together at last, admit they should have divorced years ago — and, in the novel's final image of them, walk away from Yesteryear Ranch hand in hand

Discussion Topics

  1. First impressions of Natalie. The opening chapter shows her narrating her own discipline while her staff and kids are already up doing the work. How did you feel about her from the start — and has that shifted by Chapter 30?
  2. What's actually happening in 1855? Time travel, a head injury, a fever dream, a hidden reality show, a religious test — the section headers hint that "present" and "past" mean something different than they first appear. What's your theory now, and what clues are you tracking?
  3. The "MAMA, 1855" carving. That single image does a lot of work early on. What did that moment do for you as a reader — horror, confusion, dark humor?
  4. Curated domesticity vs. the real thing. Compare the filmed sourdough tutorial in Part One with Mary and Maeve's actual, exhausting chores in 1855. What does the contrast say about the "tradwife" aesthetic Natalie sells?
  5. "I was a shark, and they were tiny fish." Natalie's private contempt for her own followers is one of the book's sharpest lines. Does it change how you think about influencer culture generally — or about specific accounts you follow?
  6. Early red flags in the courtship. Natalie and Caleb are engaged within weeks, largely around a shared fantasy of farm life. Looking back, what did you notice in the courtship chapters that you might have missed on a first read?
  7. Is the satire landing? Yesteryear has been described (and reviewed) as satire of tradwife culture, but reviews are split on whether it's effective. Is it working for you so far, or does it feel heavy-handed?
  8. Mary and the power dynamic. Mary is a child, yet she stitches Natalie's wound, dictates chores, and insists on her own authority in the house. What do you make of her role now that we've seen her disappear and come back shaking (Chapter 27)?
  9. Eliza's "imagine you're being watched." This throwaway coping mechanism from Natalie's mother becomes the entire foundation of her influencer career. What does it say about where Natalie's performance of self actually comes from?
  10. The ranch, bought with a condition. Doug funds the $5 million purchase only if Natalie gives Caleb "a big American family." How does this reframe the "traditional family" the ranch is built to project — as a business deal from day one?
  11. The sauce baster scene. Natalie's self-insemination — clinical, joyless, and hidden — sits behind everything she later sells online as natural, easy fertility. Discuss the gap between the labor of building a family and the aesthetic of one.
  12. Chapter 29. This is one of the darkest chapters so far. How did the book's satire hold up here — does humor have any place next to this kind of scene, or does the tone shift entirely?
  13. The livestreamer and the follower surge. A stranger's endorsement — not anything Natalie does differently — is what launches her from a few thousand followers to 300,000 overnight. What does this say about who actually controls an influencer's success?
  14. Mary's disappearance (Chapter 27) and the mink in the trap (Chapter 31). What do you think she actually saw or did in the woods? Start collecting theories now — this is clearly being set up to pay off later.
  15. Cassidy's line: "You look like you're pretending." Chapter 32 has one of Natalie's own coached followers naming exactly what she's doing on camera — and Natalie's response is a joke ("Isn't everyone?"). What does it mean that she can say this out loud and still not change anything?
  16. The name that slips out (Chapter 33). Natalie blurts "Shannon... Clementine—" to Mary mid-crisis, and Mary doesn't recognize the second name at all. What do you make of a mother's own children not knowing about each other?
  17. The livestreamer and the follower surge (Chapter 34). A stranger's endorsement — not anything Natalie does differently — is what launches her from a few thousand followers to 300,000 overnight. What does this say about who actually controls an influencer's success?
  18. Abel's "initiation" (Chapter 35). The boys are being raised into very different roles depending on their gender, even inside the family's private mythology. What did this chapter reveal about how deep the performance goes when there's no audience at all?
  19. Shannon's arc so far. She starts (Chapter 38) admiring Natalie's escape from "corporate life," and by Chapter 40 is telling her the ranch is "cursed." What is Shannon starting to see that Natalie won't let herself see?
  20. The bearded neighbors (Chapter 41). Natalie's visceral, unexplained reaction to two strangers is one of the eeriest beats yet. What's your theory — and what specifically is she recognizing?
  21. Being watched vs. being seen (Chapter 43). For 40+ chapters Natalie has coped by imagining an audience watching her. Chapter 43 is the first time she admits real people around her actually matter to her. Is that progress, or just a new performance?
  22. The dinner scene (Chapter 45). Natalie says out loud, to witnesses, that she's being held against her will — and nothing happens. Why do you think no one reacts? What would it take to actually break through here?
  23. Shannon vs. Clementine. Natalie spends chapters watching Shannon for signs of betrayal and completely misses her own daughter, who turns out to be the real source gathering evidence (Chapter 52). What does that blind spot say about who Natalie has ever really seen as a threat — or as a person?
  24. The choking scene and the interview (Chapters 48 and 54). Natalie's assault on Shannon gets reframed on air as something "erotic in nature." How did the book handle the gap between what actually happened and how it gets packaged for an audience — and does it land as satire, horror, or both?
  25. Eliza's confession (Chapter 52). The lie that shaped Natalie's whole understanding of family and fidelity turns out to be her mother's, not her father's. Does this recontextualize anything about Natalie's own choices — or is it too late by that point?
  26. MANOSPHERE, carved into a doorframe (Chapter 53). What did it do for you, finally seeing where "the men" go — and finding out it's just a cabin with a mini-fridge and a football game?
  27. The reveal itself (Part Three). Once you know the "1855" world was a hoax Natalie and Caleb built deliberately to hide from prosecution, does anything about the earlier chapters read differently in hindsight? What clues do you wish you'd caught?
  28. "You're not pregnant, Mother. You're going through menopause." Natalie's entire internal narration for 50+ chapters treated her body's signals as religious and reproductive. What does this final correction do to how you read everything that came before it?
  29. Walking away hand in hand. The novel's last image of Natalie and Caleb together is neither punishment nor reconciliation — just two people who admit they should have left each other years ago, still leaving together. What do you make of that choice as an ending for their marriage?
  30. The Book of Mary (Epilogue). Mary's memoir gives the novel's final word to the one child the story spent the least time with directly. Why do you think Burke chose Mary, specifically, to write the family's ending — rather than Clementine, who did most of the actual rescuing?
  31. Reena's arc. From humiliated Harvard roommate to the journalist Natalie handpicks for her only prison interview, Reena's entire trajectory runs parallel to Natalie's collapse. What do you make of the choice to end the book with Reena holding the microphone?
  32. Looking back at the satire. Now that you know the full shape of the book, do you think Yesteryear ultimately worked as satire of tradwife/influencer culture — or did the plot mechanics (the hoax, the abuse charges) end up overtaking the social commentary?
