Step 2 · 8 min

The story so far

Tap a chapter to open a short reminder. Only what we read in the sample.

The narrator introduces himself from prison. He has been called a thief, a rebel, an upstart and a traitor, and he is Prisoner 83A0956. Most people call him Batter, after a dog he never met. He is an identical twin, and his brother was stillborn.

In the spring of 1967, Batter is ten. His mother, Rainey, is a type designer and his father, John, is a historian. Batter finds his mother with a pile of capsules and learns they are sugar pills: she takes them so his anxious father feels better.

Mrs. Barksdale asks the fourth graders what they will be, not do, when they grow up. Batter says a poet, and some children laugh. On the museum trip she tells the story of the dodo and explains extinction.

Batter’s mother explains why Helvetica is boring, and why poetry is the kind of thing that “didn’t rate a serif.” Batter learns why his parents want financial stability for him, and that being world-famous brings its own problems.

Batter goes from A. A. Milne to Bukowski in five years. His mother says a typeface can change how we see a poem. Batter says his ear for poetry was built through practice, and his father says no one “goes into” poetry.

It is 1975 and Batter arrives at a New England university. His roommate, Paul, already knows his future: the Senate before thirty. Paul has opinions about everything, including poetry, and every college student must declare a major.

Batter’s adviser, Dr. Doug Payne, tells him to make a flight plan and writes “Math major” on his form. Paul charms Batter’s parents on the phone and invents a Neuroscience story to help Batter. Batter still has not written any poetry.

In the library, Batter remembers Issue 274 of a rare quarterly, Peck & Peck, which his school librarian once let him read. The cover always shows a quail egg. The first poem he reads in it is “Come Home.”

Paul bribes the room lottery people and gets them a suite. He could join a fraternity, but he prefers Batter’s company. They begin to share stories about their families.

In sophomore year Batter becomes the person to ask for help with a paper, but he gets no dates. He tries many subjects, keeps returning to Peck & Peck, and finds that Advanced Poetic Theory feels like a math class.

Dr. Payne lists all of Batter’s majors so far and says he was a graduate student with Batter’s father, who “quit everything, too.” Then he hands Batter a flyer for a Poetry Writing Workshop.

Batter applies to the workshop and writes that he has been writing since age seven. He gets in, but the sessions are painful and he drops out and declares pre-law. Then he hears that Peck & Peck has gone out of business.

Batter promises himself to become a serious person, but he is soon back to poetry. He uses poems to understand hardship, politics and feelings, while Paul calls it annoying and elitist.

Paul plans law school at Georgetown and expects Batter to join him. Batter panics and tells his parents he has a job in New York City, which he does not. He feels he went to college to become something and became nothing.

Step 4 · 5 min

Big topics to talk about

Poetry or a safe job?

Batter’s parents want “financial stability.” Batter wants to be a poet. Whose dream should win?

The power of typefaces

Rainey believes the shape of letters changes how we feel about words. Do fonts really change meaning?

White lies and self-deception

Sugar pills, a made-up girlfriend, a made-up job. When does a small lie become a big problem?

Who tells history?

Batter’s father says history depends on who is telling it. Can we ever know the truth of the past?

Practice versus talent

Batter says an ear is built “through practice, not gifts.” Do you agree?

A mysterious magazine

Peck & Peck has no pictures, one egg on every cover, and only an issue number. Why would anyone make a magazine like this?
